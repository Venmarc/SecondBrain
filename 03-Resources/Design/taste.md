# taste.md — Victor
Last updated: 2026-10-11 | Revision: 1

## 1. Domain
What I make: Websites. I design and build websites, while also designing and building AI systems as a sidequest. Web design is my focus.

Who it's for: Peers and companies. I have no professional work yet, but I am building portfolio projects. One is currently a WIP.

What "good" gets judged against in my field: A website is a first impression. It should sell the product, skill, vision, person, team, or company even when nobody is present to explain it. It should introduce what it is quickly, then reveal more as the visitor scrolls. It should answer “why us, why it, why me” through its content and results. It should look like it sells what it sells, work on mobile and desktop, load and respond without lag, and use effects that fit its design direction. “u did more where u were supposed to do less” is a warning sign.

## 2. Taste Anchors
5-10 works I genuinely love. The WHY is the data, the list is just the index.

- **fin.com**: Loved because it taught me about Three.js, globes, depth sandwiches, and motion triggered by page interactions. It has a better definition of depth than LlamaLama. It uses layers to make the page communicate without feeling flat. The visuals and interactions keep the site interesting while still serving its direction.
- **antigravity.google**: I love the use of white and whitespace. The whole site is white with little other colors and crazy motion components, but it is not blinding. Everything earns its place or is placed correctly. There is no crowdiness or feeling that “this space is cramped but I dunno why.” The particle swarm moves like “a school of fish made of jellyfish.” It twinkles, grows, shrinks, changes color, responds to the cursor, and gives the bare hero “summ to focus on.” It makes the particles look alive. The site also hides the top bar while scrolling down and brings it back while scrolling up, works on mobile, and keeps its quiet visual direction.
- **lamalama.com**: “lamalama.com is teh goat.” It is a digital studio’s show of excellence across photography, brand design, and web design. I love the dark gray, white, and black palette; grainy particles; blocks; image and video layout; pixel jelly image distortion; pixel trail; scrambling button text; and the logo intro that dissolves into the page. The intro feels like “u are warped into their world.” It is “controlled chaos”: bold, creative, unique, standout, and eyecatching, but not overdone. The effects fit their story and feel purposeful. The site can be cleanly made without feeling clean or quiet.
- **white-desert.com**: Loved because it uses depth as part of the story. White Desert takes visitors from Cape Town to Antarctica, and the site reveals that world through vertical and horizontal movement, layered images, video, and a white haze that represents snow. It talks to the visitor in layers: “we do this,” “and this,” “because of this,” and “that’s why we are White Desert.” The depth is not only decoration. It keeps visitors interested while showing them around.
- **unive.ai**: Loved because it communicates clearly and loads fast. The hero image contains a lot of detail but appears quickly. The whitespace in the cloud gives the hero text and CTA a natural place. Feature sections use real-looking inactive UI components instead of flat screenshots, so the text, buttons, layout, and icons stay smooth. The FAQ closes one answer when another opens. The footer stays plain instead of adding another dramatic treatment. The site shows that performance, structure, and visual quality can work together.
- **paj.cash**: Loved because it is “visually active but still calm.” White is the baseline. Blue sky, clouds, image collages, typography, a mockup entering the frame, flipping images, and a focused cursor trail communicate what PAJ does without making the site noisy. The page uses motion and imagery to explain the product quickly. It is outstanding without feeling loud or confused.

## 3. Anti-References
Work that is competent, even praised, that I would never make.

- **Domain.com and api.artbloom.tech**: They look good, but I do not see myself making them. Their thick, high-contrast border treatment is not my current direction. On my own builds, unnecessary gray borders looked rough and accidental. I do not reject brutalism itself. I reject the roughness that appears when an agent adds borders without a clear direction.
- **Thick-border neo-brutalist routes**: I study them as references, but I would not choose them today. Gumroad and Buffalo Wild Wings helped me move from “I don't like this” to “it's not so bad,” but I still see them as styles to study rather than styles to use. I can respect a style without wanting to make it.
- **My own old vibe-coded webapps from about five months ago**: They are anti-references because agents added borders and high-contrast decisions without considering their effects. They looked like the design had no direction and could not remove what was unnecessary.

## 4. Judgment Questions
The questions I actually run when deciding something is good:

1. How does the website introduce itself: the product, portfolio, person, team, or company?
2. Does the first one to three sections give me a glimpse of what it is and why it matters?
3. Does it communicate value before the visitor loses interest?
4. Does it answer “why us, why it, why me” through its content and results?
5. Does it look like it sells what it sells?
6. Does the visual direction match the promise? Does anything fight the product, such as bright red for a website promising good sleep?
7. Does the site reveal more as I scroll, instead of putting the whole website in my face at once?
8. Does motion carry the story, or is it only there to grab attention?
9. Does the motion make the site move in its world without unnecessarily making the user's eyes move?
10. Do the effects belong to the same world, or did someone add them because they looked cool somewhere else?
11. Is the design controlled, or did it cross into “u did more where u were supposed to do less”?
12. Does it still work on mobile and desktop, load quickly, and respond without lag or strange displacement?

## 5. Instant-No List
Dealbreakers. If present, the work is dead regardless of everything else:

- Gradients everywhere: on text, backgrounds, cards, and every other surface. Gradients can be done right, but too much makes the site look like typical AI slop.
- Generic fonts for headings, especially Inter. There are thousands of fonts, and one of them could work better for the header and body.
- The repeated AI escape from generic design: beige or cream, big serifs, and the same “non-generic” treatment every other agent uses.
- Effects added without direction, abstraction, or a reason. “u did more where u were supposed to do less.”
- High-contrast borders that look unnecessary, rough, or accidental.
- A website that does not look like it sells what it sells.
- Lag, broken responsive behavior, strange displacement, or interactions that make the visitor lose interest before the website communicates its purpose.

## 6. Signature Moves
Patterns I return to on purpose, not by accident:

- Thin hairline borders for page sections and internal demarcations. They should be almost invisible until the visitor looks closely.
- Borderless headers and headers without a separate background color when the design direction supports it.
- Sticky headers that hide while scrolling down and reappear while scrolling up. Sometimes the header stays visible. Sometimes there is no separate header, and the navigation sits in the top corners of the hero.
- Buttons with no border when the color should reach the edge. Springy buttons. State changes on hover. Cursor feedback that shows what is clickable or interactive.
- Depth sandwiches: layered image cards, moving carousels, image cards over text or background, and content that comes to the visitor instead of making the visitor search for it.
- Scroll-based reveals that show a product or story in stages. “U use motion to make the site move in its world without necessarily making the user's eyes move.”
- Motion and effects that fit the design direction instead of being copied because they looked good on another site.
- Clear visual hierarchy and familiar interaction patterns. I return to Fitts's law, Hick's law, the Zeigarnik effect, Jakob's law, the goal-gradient effect, the von Restorff effect, and Miller's law because they reduce how much the user has to think.
- Important actions should be easy to reach. Unnecessary choices should be reduced. Progress should be visible. Similar things should stay in the same place. The user should not have to hold the entire product in their head at once.
- I try to learn and implement something new in every project. My agents pick out many details while I communicate the idea and direction behind the build.

## 7. Tension Log
Where my taste fights what performs or what clients ask for:

- **Personal taste versus conversion**: When a design I dislike produces a clear conversion gain, conversion wins. I look deeper into the role the design played, see why it worked, and may accept it or merge the reason it worked with what I already have. “I don't judge, i just look, see why, accept or agree to disagree, steal why it worked (my fave).”
- **Cool effect versus project direction**: I used pixel distortion for the images on a fictional biotech company homepage that was supposed to feel clean. It looked good at first, but it belonged to a different design style. I replaced it with a simpler effect: the right edge stayed fixed while the left side swung slightly, like a door opening. The simpler movement matched a company that made human cloning feel simpler.
- **Motion preference versus hardware limits**: I appreciate motion, but my older computer with extended RAM cannot handle some moving sites as well as a newer machine. I avoid heavy Framer Motion when possible and use CSS instead. The site should still work for devices that are not up to par.
- **A complete design library versus learning by building**: My design library is still in pieces. I keep returning to known patterns, but I also want each new project to teach me something new.

## 8. Undefined Zones
Topics marked [UNDEFINED] in the interview. My taste here does not exist yet. AI: when work touches these zones, ASK me instead of defaulting to best practices. Every “I don't know” answered with best practices makes my work look more like everyone else's.

- **[UNDEFINED] The exact feeling created by the Antigravity particle swarm.** I can describe the behavior and its effect: it looks alive, feels intriguing, resembles a cat chasing a toy, and gives the bare hero something to focus on. I cannot define the exact feeling.
- **[UNDEFINED] The exact boundary between clean, quiet, bold, controlled chaos, and noisy.** I know that Antigravity feels clean and quiet, LlamaLama feels bold and controlled, and PAJ feels visually active but calm. I cannot yet turn those boundaries into complete rules.
- **[UNDEFINED] Which exact effects, typefaces, layouts, and visual directions fit each kind of product.** I know when choices fight a promise, but I cannot yet specify the full set of combinations that work for each product.
- **[UNDEFINED] How to tell when several effects belong to the same world instead of becoming decoration.** My current rule is that the effects should fit the story and direction, but I do not yet have a reliable test.
- **[UNDEFINED] The full map of brutalist and neo-brutalist styles.** I understand some routes better now, especially Gumroad and Buffalo Wild Wings, but I am still learning which visual choices belong to each route.

## 10. Session-derived visual judgment

- Repeating one treatment across every section can make a polished page read as a template.
- A critic score is capped by weak photography, unclear page structure, or a visual direction that does not match the brief.
- Freeze a trusted baseline before isolated visual iteration. Keep experiments in a separate worktree or copy.
- A footer, mascot, or effect must have a job in the story or information hierarchy. Do not add it because the reference looked interesting.
- A real-feeling fictional surface keeps process labels and implementation notes out of the UI. Those belong in project notes and conversation.
